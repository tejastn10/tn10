I've finished this collection of Rohan Chakravarty's *Green Humour* comics — the strip that has
become India's most recognisable voice for wildlife and environmental cartooning. It's a fat,
colourful anthology of single panels, short strips, mock field guides and fake nature
documentaries, all built on a foundation of genuinely careful natural history. The humour is
pun-heavy and often savage, but it never comes at the cost of the facts.

## Themes I Noticed

**Conservation as politics, not sentiment**

- Habitat loss, mining clearances, linear infrastructure (roads, canals, power lines) cutting
  through forests and corridors
- The gap between environmental policy on paper and enforcement on the ground
- How "development vs. wildlife" is usually a false binary hiding bad planning

**Climate change with a straight face**

- Carbon footprints, fossil-fuel lobbying, and the theatre of international climate summits
- Melting ice, shifting ranges, and species running out of room to migrate to
- The absurdity of treating the atmosphere as someone else's problem

**India's underdogs get the spotlight**

- Not just tigers and elephants — the Great Indian Bustard, gharials, hornbills, Amur falcons,
  vultures, dugongs, the creatures that rarely make the poster
- Human–wildlife conflict framed from the animal's point of view
- Local communities as conservation partners rather than obstacles

**Satire aimed upward**

- Politicians, corporations and greenwashing campaigns are the recurring targets
- Ridicule as a way of making dense ecology and policy land with a general audience
- The cartoonist as a low-cost, high-reach form of environmental journalism

## Memorable Lines

The strips lean on wordplay — species names bent into puns, wildlife "interviewed" like
celebrities, IPCC reports narrated like sports commentary. A few recurring beats that stuck:

- Animals filing complaints about their shrinking real estate
- "Bird of the week" features that double as quiet obituaries for declining species
- Summit delegates congratulating each other while the graph behind them keeps climbing

## Final Thoughts

What makes the book work is the ratio: every joke is anchored to a real species, a real
policy, or a real report. It's an easy, funny read that leaves you better informed about Indian
wildlife and environmental policy than most long-form pieces would — and angrier, in a useful
way. Good palate cleanser between heavier books, and a reminder that clear communication about
hard subjects is a craft in itself.

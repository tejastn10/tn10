I've finished this short, gleefully absurd story about a father who pops out for milk when his kids'
cereal runs dry, and comes back late with an outlandish explanation: he was abducted by aliens,
handed off to pirates, rescued by a time-traveling stegosaurus, and generally swept through one
escalating catastrophe after another—all, somehow, without ever letting go of the milk. Told entirely
through the father's telling of it to his skeptical children, the book is one long shaggy-dog story
that keeps doubling back on itself in the best way.

## Themes I Noticed

**The Tall Tale as a Bonding Ritual**

- The children's running interruptions and demands for consistency turn storytelling into a shared
  game rather than a monologue
- Exaggeration as a father's way of turning an ordinary errand into something worth remembering
- The milk itself as the one fixed point holding an increasingly ridiculous plot together

**Time Loops and Narrative Mischief**

- A time-traveling stegosaurus who causes the very problem the story needs solving
- Cause and effect looping back on themselves until the "explanation" explains nothing at all
- Gaiman using a children's book to sneak in a genuinely clever structural joke

**Everyday Errands, Extraordinary Excuses**

- The gap between "I was late" and the increasingly baroque reasons why
- How childhood logic accepts escalating nonsense as long as it's told with total conviction
- A gentle nod to every parent who has ever stretched the truth to entertain, not deceive

## Memorable Quotes

"I got the milk from some green globby aliens who wanted to turn the Earth into a golf course."

"'Dad,' I said. 'The stegosaurus doesn't have a hot air balloon.' 'It does now,' said my father."

"Fortunately, I had the milk."

Chris Riddell's illustrations do a lot of the comic heavy lifting here, giving every dinosaur,
alien, and pirate a manic, cartoonish energy that matches Gaiman's escalating nonsense line for line.
It's a quick read—barely more than an afternoon—but it's dense with the kind of inventive, rule-of-cool
plotting that makes it fun for adults reading along as much as for kids. Nothing about it is meant to
hang together logically, and that's exactly the point: it's a celebration of storytelling for its own
sake, delivered with a completely straight face.
